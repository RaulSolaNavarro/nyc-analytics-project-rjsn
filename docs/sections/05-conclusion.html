Conclusion & Results

Summary of Findings

This project set out to answer two core analytics questions about NYC road infrastructure using a data warehouse integrating 311 service requests and DOT automated traffic volume counts from 2020 to 2025.

Q1: Does traffic volume correlate with complaint frequency?

At the borough level, we found no clear relationship. Manhattan carries the highest average daily traffic (about 13,500 vehicles per counted street segment per day) yet ranks third in total complaints (154,282). Staten Island has the second highest traffic (about 12,300) and the fewest complaints (77,886), while Queens has the most complaints (279,093) with the third highest traffic (about 10,000). Across the five boroughs, the traffic ranking and the complaint ranking do not match.

The traffic comparison deserves caution. The DOT counted only a small number of segments, so each borough’s average rests on few observations (178 segment-days in Staten Island, 639 in the Bronx, 739 in Manhattan, 1,179 in Queens, and 1,454 in Brooklyn), and a different set of counted segments could reorder the boroughs.

This suggests that raw traffic volume is an incomplete predictor of complaint frequency. Other factors, including road age, lane miles, residential density, and reporting behavior, likely contribute, but this project did not test them.

The T2R metric (Query 8) and Peak-Load Service Lag KPI (Query 9) point the same way. Days to close shows no consistent pattern with traffic: Staten Island has the second highest traffic and the fastest closure, Brooklyn (fourth in traffic) is the slowest, and the Bronx (lowest traffic) is the second fastest. Five data points cannot support a statistical claim. For peak load, we defined peak months with a within-segment traffic index so that the result does not depend on which segments the DOT happened to count. Average closure time was 12.66 days in peak months and 12.69 days in non-peak months, with a median of 1 day in both. We observed no difference, which is not the same as proving that no lag exists, because the traffic counts are sparse and no significance test was run.

Q2: Does response time vary across boroughs?

Yes. Brooklyn takes an average of 14.1 days to close a street complaint, followed by Manhattan (13.4), Queens (12.8), and the Bronx (10.4). Staten Island closes in just 6.8 days, about half of Brooklyn’s time.

Staten Island has the fewest complaints of any borough, yet three of its ZIP codes (10314, 10306, and 10312) are in the citywide top five by complaint count. Because those are raw counts with no population or street-mile denominator, they show where complaints concentrate, not where roads are worst.

The Staten Island result is worth highlighting: it has the second highest measured traffic and the fastest closure time, the opposite of a traffic-driven lag. Whether the differences reflect staffing, complaint types, or geographic compactness requires further investigation.

Averages also hide how skewed closure times are. In the Query 9 sample the median was 1 day while the average was about 12.7 days, because a minority of tickets stay open for a very long time (the longest exceeded 1,900 days).

Recommendations for Stakeholders

For the NYC Department of Transportation:

  • Investigate why Brooklyn has the slowest average closure time (14.1 days) while ranking second in total complaints. A borough-level staffing or routing analysis could identify whether this is a resource or workflow issue.
  • Review Staten Island ZIP codes 10314, 10306, and 10312, which are in the citywide top five by complaint count. Check complaint rates per street mile or household before prioritizing preventive maintenance, since our counts are not normalized.
  • The seasonal chart shows complaints peaking in late winter and spring (March was the peak month in 2022, 2023, and 2024), which suggests that seasonal readiness planning (winter road damage followed by a spring complaint wave) could reduce peak workload.

For Logistics & Delivery Companies:

  • Queens ZIP codes 11385 and 11101 have among the highest complaint counts in the city (11,397 and 10,889). Route planning tools that incorporate 311 complaint hotspot data could help reduce vehicle maintenance costs and delays.

For Urban Planners:

  • The disconnect between traffic sensor coverage and complaint geography highlights a data gap: traffic sensors are concentrated in certain corridors, while complaints span the full residential street network. Expanding sensor coverage to residential ZIP codes with high complaint counts would enable more precise infrastructure planning.

Limitations & Data Challenges

Warning

Geographic join resolution
The 311 data geolocates complaints to ZIP codes, while the traffic data geolocates sensors to road segments with WKT geometries. Cross-mart joins are therefore only possible at the borough level, not at the street or ZIP level. A more granular spatial join (e.g., snapping complaints to the nearest sensor segment) was out of scope for this project but would substantially strengthen the analysis.

Warning

Traffic sensor coverage is sparse and uneven
The DOT traffic count dataset covers a subset of road segments, not the full street network, and only a few segments are counted in a borough on a given day (about 2.0 to 2.6 on average). Sensors are more densely placed on arterial and commercial roads, so residential streets, which generate many of the complaints in outer-borough ZIP codes, may be systematically under-counted. Because of this, traffic is measured as the average count per street segment per day, borough averages rest on as few as 178 segment-days (Staten Island), and some months have too few counts to use (Query 9 excludes months with fewer than 30 segment-days). Borough traffic rankings should therefore be treated as indicative only.

Note

Traffic counts do not cover the full period
The DOT records traffic at specific segments for specific periods; not all segments are counted every day, and several months in 2020 have no counts at all. The average daily traffic figures used in this analysis are averages over counted segment-days only, which may introduce bias toward the periods and streets the DOT chose to measure.

Note

Analysis window
All results cover complaints and traffic counts from 2020 through 2025. The extracts also contained 2026 records (38,861 street-related complaints at the time of extraction), which were excluded so that every figure describes the same six years.

Note

Missing ZIP codes
8.6% of street complaints (78,254) have no ZIP code and 0.2% (1,705) carry the invalid placeholder “10000”. Both are excluded from the ZIP-level map and rankings, which cover 91.2% of complaints (825,575). A first version of the ZIP query double-counted complaints because dim_incident_location has repeated keys; the final query de-duplicates the dimension, and the ZIP table now reconciles to the borough totals.

Note

311 reporting behavior varies by community
Complaint counts reflect both actual infrastructure conditions and the willingness of residents to file 311 reports. Areas with higher civic engagement may show more complaints not because their roads are worse, but because residents are more likely to report issues.

Note

Five boroughs, no significance testing
Borough-level comparisons rest on five data points, and no statistical tests were run. The findings are descriptive.

Recommended Follow-Up Analysis

  1. Street-segment level spatial join: Use PostGIS or BigQuery GIS functions to snap 311 complaints to their nearest DOT sensor segment, enabling correlation analysis at a finer geographic grain.

  2. Complaint type decomposition: Analyze whether Street Condition complaints (a category that includes potholes among other defects) track differently from Street Light Condition complaints relative to traffic volume, since road surface damage is more directly caused by vehicle load.

  3. Year-over-year response time trend: Query 2 produces a static borough average. Adding a year dimension would show whether response times are improving or degrading over the 2020–2025 window.

  4. Heavy vehicle sub-analysis: The original proposal targeted heavy vehicle counts (trucks) specifically. The DOT dataset includes vehicle class data that was not fully leveraged here; a filtered analysis on heavy vehicles vs. road surface complaints would better test the infrastructure-degradation hypothesis.

  5. Normalized complaint rates: Divide ZIP-level complaint counts by street miles or households so that ZIP codes can be compared on rate, not just on volume.
